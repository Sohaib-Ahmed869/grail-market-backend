What the system does 02

Three questions, answered in order

Each stage depends on the one before it. Getting the printing wrong makes every number after it meaningless, so identification comes first.

Stage one

Which card is this?

Down to the set and the collector number. Two cards can share a name and differ in value a thousandfold, so this is settled before anything else.

Stage two

What condition is it in?

Centering measured from the printed border and compared to the PSA standard. Surface marks found and shown on the card itself.

Stage three

What is it worth?

Graded value if it is in a slab, raw value if it is not — taken from real completed sales and converted to Australian dollars.

The commercial point

A card in a grading slab is worth its graded price. Most price guides quote the raw, ungraded figure. For the card on slide 06 that difference is A$5,580 against A$19,949 — the same object, described two ways.

What it does not claim

The system identifies a card; it does not authenticate one. It can tell you which card this is meant to be, not that it is genuine. High-value cards should still go to a grading company.

The system on one page 03

How the pieces fit together

Condition is measured on our own infrastructure and never leaves it. Only the card's identity and its price reach outside, and both are stored so the same card is never looked up twice.

Stage two · measuring condition 04

How the grading actually works

Centering is a measurement, not an opinion, and this is the sequence that produces it.

1

Find the card and correct the angle

A card is 63 × 88 mm, so anything whose shape falls outside a sensible window around that ratio is a table edge or a sleeve, not the card. Once found, the perspective is corrected onto a fixed canvas — a card photographed at an angle then measures the same as one shot straight down.

2

Decide whether the photo can be trusted at all

Focus is scored, glare is measured as a percentage of the card surface, and the available detail is checked. Failing any one stops the scan with a plain reason and a re-shoot instruction, and no grade is issued.

glare above 4% of the surface · not enough detail · out of focus
3

Walk inward from each edge to find the printed border

Starting at the card edge, the colour is followed pixel by pixel inward. The moment it departs from the border colour and stays departed, the print edge has been found. Only the middle of each side is sampled, so rounded corners cannot skew the result. Four edges give four distances, and those give the centering.

sampled across the central 15–85% of each side
4

Score the measurement before reporting it

A reading is only as good as its evidence. The border ring is checked for uniformity and real contrast. Where a card has no printed border, or the lighting has destroyed it, the result is marked unmeasurable rather than published as a number — and a confidence percentage travels with every figure that is published.

confidence published 0–100% alongside the measurement
5

Compare to the professional standard and show the marks

The worst side decides it: 60/40 or better passes PSA 10, 65/35 passes PSA 9. Every scratch and print line on the surface is found and boxed on the image, so the owner can look at what was detected rather than take a score on trust.

PSA 10 ≤ 60/40 · PSA 9 ≤ 65/35
Measured

Centering — geometry taken from the printed border, with a confidence score attached to every reading.

Detected, then estimated

Corners, edges and surface — every mark is found and shown. Converting marks into a sub-grade is rule-based today, and labelled as an estimate in the product.

Stage one · identifying the printing 05

Three ways to name a card, best first

The system stops at the first method that gives a confident answer, so the most reliable evidence always wins.

1

Read the grading label

A graded card carries its own answer key. The label prints the year, the set and the collector number, and those three together resolve to exactly one card — no name matching and no interpretation. This is tried first on every scan.

2

Match the card itself against the catalogues

For raw cards, the text on the card is read and eight game catalogues are searched at once. The photograph is then compared against each catalogue's own artwork to settle which printing it is.

3

Fall back to an assisted match — then confirm it

For steep angles, heavy glare or Japanese printings, an image model proposes a name. That proposal is never shown on its own: it goes back through the real catalogue, and only a confirmed catalogue entry reaches the screen.

Where none of the three produces a confident answer, the system reports what it read off the card and shows no price — rather than attaching a plausible figure from the wrong set.

How the system handles doubt 09

What it will not do

A valuation tool is only useful if its numbers can be relied on. These are the limits the system holds to, even when holding to them means showing less.

✕

It will not grade a photograph it cannot read. A clear reason and a re-shoot instruction are more useful than a number nobody should trust.

✕

It will not let an image model set a price. A model may propose a card's name, and that proposal is confirmed against a real catalogue. Prices come only from market feeds and completed sales.

✕

It will not present an estimate as a sale. Calculated figures are labelled everywhere they appear, including in the headline value.

✕

It will not price a card it cannot confirm. Where the exact printing is uncertain, no price is shown at all.

✕

It will not guess at centering with nothing to measure. Full-art cards have no printed border, and the system reports that rather than inventing a figure.

✕

It will not leave a blank unexplained. Wherever a value is missing, the interface states the reason on the card itself.
